River Crossing
Open CodeAIM ↗

The classic puzzle. One robot.

A wolf, a goat, and a cabbage all need to cross a river. The robot can only carry one at a time. Leave the wrong pair alone on a bank and somebody gets eaten. Your job: get all three across without losing anyone.

3 cargo 1 river 7 crossings

What you're doing

You'll draw a river across your field mat with an Expo marker, place the three items on the left bank, and code the robot to ferry them across one at a time. The catch: there are two combinations you can never leave alone together. Solving the order is a puzzle. Coding it is the second puzzle. You'll do this three times — once with blocks, once with Switch, once in plain Python — redrawing the river each round so your numbers have to change.

Level 1 — Blocks

Blocks
Your task

Draw a river across the field. Put the wolf, goat, and cabbage on the left bank. Solve the crossing puzzle on paper first — then code the robot in Blocks to do all 7 crossings without breaking the rules.

Drive it first — Grab the controller and drive one crossing by hand before you touch any code. Get a feel for how the robot moves and how it picks things up.

Step 1: Set up the field

Grab an Expo marker. On the field mat:

  • Draw a river all the way across. Max 130 mm (5 inches) wide. Make it a clear strip.
  • On the left bank, mark an X where each item starts: blue barrel (wolf), orange barrel (goat), sports ball (cabbage).
  • Mark a START spot for the robot on the left bank.

Place the actual items on the Xs you drew. The right bank stays empty — that's the goal.

The mat is dry-erase — the marker comes right off when you're done.

Step 2: Solve the puzzle on paper first

Before any code, work out the order of crossings with a pencil. The robot can carry one item at a time. After it drops something off, it has to come back — empty trips count too.

Left bank
Wolf Goat Cabbage
River
Right bank
empty
Check the bank after every trip — After the robot drops something off and starts back, look at who's left alone on each bank. If you see wolf+goat or goat+cabbage alone together, you lost. Pick a different order.

It takes 7 trips to solve this. Some of those trips you'll be carrying something back to the left bank — that's not a mistake, it's the trick. Items go both directions.

Write down your 7 trips before you open VEXcode. Each trip is a single direction: left→right with X or right→left with X or right→left empty.

Step 3: Measure your distances

For each trip across, you need two numbers:

  • Distance. Use your ruler. Measure from where the robot starts that trip to where it needs to drop off, in centimeters. Multiply by 10 to get millimeters. (VEX wants mm.)
  • Turn. Use your protractor on the robot. Most crossings need a turn before driving so the robot faces the right item on pick-up and the right spot on drop-off.

Write the numbers down beside each of your 7 trips. You'll need them in your code.

Step 4: Code it in Blocks

Open VEXcode AIM in Blocks mode. For each trip, your stack will look something like:

  • Turn (left or right) by your measured degrees
  • Drive forward by your measured distance in mm
  • Place the item (barrel) or kick it (ball) — on drop-off trips only

Then the next trip starts wherever the robot ended last. Stack all 7 trips end-to-end and run the whole thing.

First: How to read Python

When you turn your blocks into Switch, you'll see lines of Python like this for the first time. Don't panic. Every line in this lesson follows the same shape:

robot.move_for(450, 0)
  • Whorobot — who are you talking to? The robot.
  • .The dot means "go inside" or "use your..." — think of it as opening a toolbox.
  • Whatmove_for — what do you want it to do? Move.
  • (Open parenthesis — here come the details.
  • Details450, 0 — the specifics. 450 millimeters, 0 degrees. Commas separate them. Order matters.
  • )Close parenthesis — done.

In English: "Hey robot, use your move — 450 mm, 0 degrees."

Try it on three more

robot.turn_for(LEFT, 90)
Who: robot  ·  What: turn_for  ·  Details: LEFT, 90 degrees
"Hey robot, use your turn — left, 90 degrees."
robot.kicker.kick(MEDIUM)
Who: robot.kicker (the kicker inside the robot — two dots = two toolboxes deep)  ·  What: kick  ·  Details: MEDIUM power
"Hey robot, open your kicker toolbox, use the kick — medium power."
wait(0.5, SECONDS)
Who: nobody — this one isn't aimed at the robot  ·  What: wait  ·  Details: 0.5, SECONDS
"Pause for half a second."
Two dots? That just means you're going deeper. robot → open the robot → kicker → open the kicker toolbox → kick → use the kick tool. It's nested boxes.

Read any line

  1. Find the dots. Everything before the last dot is the Who. The word after the last dot (before the parenthesis) is the What.
  2. Look inside the parentheses. Those are the Details. Commas separate them. Order matters — the first detail is always the first thing the command needs to know.
  3. Say it out loud. "Hey [Who], do [What] with [Details]." If it sounds right in English, you probably read it right.

Step 3: Type all 7 crossings

        
Write the commands for Trip 1. Then Trip 2. All the way to Trip 7. Trip 1 looks something like this:

        


# Trip 1: take the goat across (left -> right)
robot.turn_for(RIGHT, 30)        # face the goat, your degrees
robot.move_for(200, 0)         # drive to the goat, mm
# ... pick up goat (carry slot)
robot.turn_for(LEFT, 90)         # face the right bank
robot.move_for(500, 0)         # drive across the river
robot.kicker.place()           # drop the goat on the right bank

        

        
For empty return trips (no item on board), skip the place/kick line. The cabbage (sports ball) is the only one that uses kick() instead of place() — both barrels use place().

        
Each trip starts wherever the robot ended last. Trip 2's distances and turns are measured from where Trip 1 dropped off, not from the original start.

1. The pain you just felt

          
Look at the code you wrote in Level 3. You wrote 7 trips. Same shape every time: turn, drive, sometimes place. Different numbers. When you redrew the river in Level 2, you had to change every single one of those numbers by hand.

          
Talk it through with your partner: what's the same across all 7 trips? What's different?


          

            The answer —
            The shape is the same every time: turn, drive, maybe place. What's different is the numbers (direction, degrees, distance) and whether the robot is carrying something to drop off.
          

        


        

          
2. What's a variable?

          
A variable is a name for a value. Instead of writing 450 all over your code, you write distance, and you decide once what distance means.

          


# Before (number hardcoded)
robot.move_for(450, 0)

# After (with a variable)
distance = 450
robot.move_for(distance, 0)

          

          
That looks like more code, not less. But hold on — what if you had seven different distances?

        


        

          
3. What's a loop?

          
A loop means: do this thing more than once. Instead of writing the same trip code seven times, write it once and tell the robot "do this for each trip."

          
Think of your 7 trips as a checklist. A loop checks off each item, does the work, then moves to the next one.


          


# A list of three things
cargo_list = ["wolf", "goat", "cabbage"]

# Do something with each one
for cargo in cargo_list:
    robot.screen.print(cargo)

          

          
This prints each name one at a time. The loop does the repeating for you.

        


        

          
4. Build it

          
Now put it together. Each trip has four things: a turn direction, degrees, a distance, and an action (either "place" for a drop-off, or "none" for an empty return trip). Stack them into a list of 7 trips.

          
Step by step:

          
    
            
  1. Make a list with your 7 trips' info: (turn_direction, degrees, distance, action).
    2. 
            
  2. Write the trip code once, using variable names instead of real numbers.
    3. 
            
  3. Wrap it in a for loop. Use an if so the place command only fires when there's something to drop off.
    4. 
          


          
Here's the shape — fill in your own numbers:

          


# Each trip: (direction, degrees, distance_mm, action)
# action is "place" if dropping something off, "none" for an empty return
trips = [
    (RIGHT, 30, 500, "place"),  # 1: goat -> right
    (LEFT,  90, 500, "none"),   # 2: empty back
    (RIGHT, 45, 520, "place"),  # 3: wolf -> right
    (LEFT,  90, 520, "place"),  # 4: goat back to left
    (RIGHT, 60, 540, "place"),  # 5: cabbage -> right
    (LEFT,  90, 540, "none"),   # 6: empty back
    (RIGHT, 30, 500, "place"),  # 7: goat -> right
]

for direction, degrees, distance, action in trips:
    robot.turn_for(direction, degrees)
    robot.move_for(distance, 0)

    if action == "place":
        robot.kicker.place()

          

          
Replace the numbers in the list with your real measurements. Don't touch the loop code below it. If your cabbage trip needs kick() instead of place(), use "kick" as the action and add another if for it.

        


        

          
5. The payoff

          
Remember Level 2, when you had to fix every number by hand for all 7 trips on a new river?

          
With a loop, you'd only change the numbers in one place — the list at the top. The driving code never changes.


          

            That's the whole point —
            Variables and loops aren't fancy computer-science magic. They're what you do when you notice you're typing the same thing over and over. You had the problem first. Now you have the fix.
          

        


        

          
Stuck?

          


            

              

                
?My loop only runs once.

                
              

              

                

                  Try first
                  Count the items in your list. A loop runs once per item. If your list has one item, you get one run.
                

                

                  Then this
                  Check indentation. Every line inside the for must be indented the same (use 4 spaces). Lines that aren't indented are outside the loop.
                

                

                  Approach
                  Put robot.screen.print(action) as the first line inside the loop. Now you can see the loop run on the robot screen. If you see 7 actions print, the loop is fine and the bug is somewhere else.
                

                

              

            


            

              

                
?My variables are doing weird things.

                
              

              

                

                  Try first
                  Check the order. The names in the for line have to match the order of values in your list. If you wrote (direction, degrees, distance, cargo) but unpacked as distance, direction, degrees, cargo, your numbers are in the wrong slots.
                

                

                  Then this
                  Pick one trip. Print all four values on the robot screen before it moves. If the screen shows numbers in the wrong slots, your list and your unpack don't match.
                

                

                  Approach
                  Comment out everything except one trip in your list. Get that one running with variables. Then add the second trip. Build up one trip at a time.
                

                

              

            


            

              

                
?The place command fires on empty return trips.

                
              

              

                

                  Try first
                  Check your if line. What word are you comparing? Does it exactly match the action word in your list ("place" vs "none")?
                

                

                  Then this
                  Two equals signs: ==, not =. One equals sign assigns a value, two equals signs checks if things match.
                

                

                  Approach
                  Print the action right before the if: robot.screen.print(action). Look at the screen each trip. Now check your if against what you just saw — they have to be the exact same word.
